# The ICP test

Every ad gets judged as the buyer, not as the person who made it. Run this
before anything ships.

## Sit in the chair first

Eden's buyer is a real estate team leader with agents to feed. They are already
paying a portal, an agency or a lead vendor. Their job is putting qualified
conversations in front of their team so agents produce. They have been pitched
this exact thing before and been let down.

They are scrolling at 7am between showings. **You get about half a second.**

Read every ad in that chair and answer one question honestly: *would I stop,
and would I click?* Not "is this clever". Not "is this on brand". Not "is this
a format nobody has used". Would this specific tired person, mid-scroll, stop.

## The five checks

An ad should pass at least three. Ads that pass one are art.

1. **Recognition.** Does it show something from their actual week? Their invoice,
   their CRM, a lead sold to four agents, the 10pm follow-up they do themselves.
   Recognition beats persuasion: people stop at their own life.
2. **Specificity.** A real number, a real timeframe, a real object. "25
   appointments in one month" stops a scroll. "Consistent qualified clients"
   does not. Vague is invisible.
3. **Outcome, not mechanism.** Say what changes for their team, not how the
   system works. Qualification, routing and instant response are the plane
   flight. More closings without working nights is the holiday.
4. **A reason to act now.** An offer, a free thing, a slot that runs out, a risk
   reversal. Most of these ads have none, and an ad with no offer is a poster.
5. **No decoding required.** If the reader has to work out a metaphor before the
   meaning arrives, the ad has already lost. Clever costs a beat, and a beat is
   the whole budget.

## Where this goes wrong, every time

**The most creative ad is usually the weakest.** A metaphor made literal — a
slot machine, a vending machine of contacts, a wall of missing posters — wins
the internal review and loses the feed, because the person who made it already
knows the answer and the buyer does not.

Warning signs an ad is for the maker rather than the buyer:

- It needs a caption to explain the visual.
- Its best quality is that it is unexpected.
- It agitates a pain and never says what to do about it.
- It is about the ad itself, or about the company, rather than about the
  reader's team and their numbers.
- It looks like a designer made it. The best performers usually do not.

## Interrupt is not enough

Interrupting is the easy half. An ad that stops the scroll and offers no next
step spends money buying attention and then discards it. Pair every interrupt
with something concrete: a number they can check, an outcome they want, or an
offer they can take today.

## The rule

Judge from the chair, not the studio. If the ad only works once explained, it
does not work.
